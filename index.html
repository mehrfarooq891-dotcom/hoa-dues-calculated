How HOA Dues Are Calculated: Budget Divided by Homes

There is no mystery formula behind your HOA bill. In simple terms, the board estimates what the community will cost to run for the year — operations plus reserve savings — and divides that total across the homes. The details differ by property type, and those details explain why two neighbours can pay different amounts.

Step 1: the annual budget

The board estimates upcoming costs: utility bills, vendor contracts (landscaping, pool, waste), insurance premiums, management, and administration. That operating budget is the starting point for every assessment.

Step 2: divide by units — or by size

In a single-family subdivision, associations usually bill every home an identical flat rate — HOAFeeCalculator.com gives $250 a month as a typical example — regardless of lot size or bedroom count. In a condominium, dues are frequently set by square-footage percentage: an owner in an 1,800-square-foot penthouse pays more than a neighbour in a 750-square-foot one-bedroom unit, because their share of the building is larger.

Step 3: add the reserve contribution

Reserve studies, carried out by professional engineers, project the cost and timing of big future jobs. If an engineer projects a $300,000 elevator modernisation in five years, the board must save $60,000 a year toward it — and that saving is built into today's monthly dues. Reserve funds typically take 15% to 40% of each monthly assessment.

Why dues rise

Boards routinely raise assessments each year to match inflation, utility rates, vendor wages and insurance premiums. Some states and community documents cap how much a board can raise dues without a membership vote — California's Davis-Stirling Act, for example, is described by HOAFeeCalculator.com as capping board-approved increases at 20% — but anything above a cap, or an emergency shortfall, generally needs a formal vote.
